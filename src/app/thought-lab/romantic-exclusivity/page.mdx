# Romantic exclusivity as structural necessity

I accept that demands for romantic exclusivity can become coercive. I ask whether every such demand is only a social habit. Some forms of commitment may depend on obligations to one particular person.

I explain the structure of my published argument here. For the full argument and references, read my [peer-reviewed article](https://doi.org/10.3390/philosophies10050102).

## The tension beneath the argument

I take concerns about restriction seriously. A demand for exclusivity can appear to limit one partner's freedom for the other's benefit. I also see commitment as a way to recognize a person who cannot simply be replaced by someone else.

I distinguish **possession** from **particularized obligation**, which means an obligation to a particular person. Treating a partner as property makes exclusivity morally indefensible. Treating a partner as replaceable loses the personal commitment that the relationship claims to honor.

## Three philosophical pressures

### Kant: respect for persons

I use Kantian respect to reject treating a partner merely as a means of satisfaction. A defense of exclusivity must preserve agency, reciprocity, and each partner's standing as an end in themselves.

### Scheler: recognizing value

I draw on Scheler to explain how love can recognize a person's value without reducing that value to preference. I do not claim that only one person has value. I argue that commitment responds to a particular person in a way that cannot be generalized without changing its meaning.

### Schopenhauer: questioning desire

I use Schopenhauer to question the stories that desire tells about itself. Biological and metaphysical forces may operate beneath those stories. This concern requires me to distinguish commitment from possession disguised as devotion.

## Structural necessity, carefully stated

I distinguish externally imposed control from exclusivity that belongs to a particular form of mutual recognition. In the second case, removing exclusivity may change the kind of relationship, rather than simply loosen its terms.

I make this claim conditionally. It does not justify surveillance, ownership, or denial of a partner's right to leave. I defend commitment only when it remains reciprocal, allows honest revision, and respects each person's dignity.
