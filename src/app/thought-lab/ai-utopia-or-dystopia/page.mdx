# AI Utopia or Dystopia? The Answer Will Be Written in Employment Contracts

I worry about workers who help create value and then lose their jobs when employers adopt AI. Discussions about the future often make these losses sound unavoidable. I want to examine the people and decisions behind them.

Executives, boards, investors, public agencies, and managers decide how institutions use AI. They can use productivity gains to expand services, shorten hours, or increase profits. I judge their choices by who benefits and who carries the risk.

I can imagine two companies using the same tools with different results. One reduces its team until the workload becomes intolerable. The other keeps its employees, provides training, and helps them solve harder problems. I expect different social consequences because the employers make different decisions.

I see a tension in the AI industry. Leading laboratories compete for human expertise while their executives predict that AI will perform more professional work. I want to understand what that tension means for workers.

## Follow the talent, carefully

I describe the available evidence as **talent migration**. “Exodus” would suggest a comprehensive movement in one direction. The evidence does not establish that wider claim.

I use SignalFire's [2025 State of Tech Talent report](https://www.signalfire.com/blog/signalfire-state-of-talent-report-2025) as one source. In its dataset, engineers were eight times more likely to move from OpenAI to Anthropic than in the reverse direction. The DeepMind-to-Anthropic ratio was nearly eleven to one. I treat these figures as specific to that population and period. They do not describe every hire, motive, or destination.

I treat retention as a separate measure. SignalFire reported second-year retention of 80% for Anthropic employees hired at least two years earlier. The corresponding figures were 78% for DeepMind and 67% for OpenAI. I read this as evidence of effective retention at Anthropic under that definition. It does not explain why employees stayed or establish whether the pattern continued into 2026.

I see continued demand for experienced researchers and engineers who define problems, evaluate evidence, find failures, and take responsibility for uncertain systems. Human judgment remains valuable within the laboratories that build tools to automate intellectual work. I think this tension needs closer examination.

## What Amodei actually predicted

I distinguish a forecast about capability from evidence of job losses. At Davos on January 20, 2026, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei described how some engineers at his company use models. They let models produce code, then edit it or do the surrounding work. He predicted that models might do “most, maybe all” software engineering work end to end within six to twelve months. I refer to the [World Economic Forum's account](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/young-people-ai-davos/) for his prediction and description of internal practice.

I need employment data to assess displacement. Code generation, task completion, demand for a profession, and employment are related but different measures. Engineers may spend more time on specification, review, security, architecture, customer communication, and accountability. Employers may also use AI to reduce team size. I see several possible responses to the same capability.

I also examine Anthropic's [company-produced study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic). It surveyed 132 engineers and researchers, conducted 53 interviews, and analyzed internal Claude Code usage. Employees reported using Claude in roughly 60% of their work. They estimated an average productivity increase of about 50%. I treat these as self-reported observations from a workplace with unusually high AI use. I would compare them with independent measures of productivity, software quality, working hours, incidents, and skill development.

I read the early labor-market evidence with similar care. An Anthropic analysis used U.S. Current Population Survey data. It found no systematic increase in unemployment among workers in occupations with high AI exposure. For workers aged 22 to 25, it estimated a 14% decline in the rate of finding jobs in those occupations.

I note that this estimate compared the period after ChatGPT's release with 2022. The authors called the estimate [“just barely statistically significant”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts). They identified alternative explanations and described the hiring evidence as tentative. I do not treat this analysis as proof of causation.

I am concerned about entry-level jobs because they give people a route into professional expertise. If firms automate junior tasks without creating new routes, short-term savings could reduce the future supply of senior professionals.

## Fable and the power to control access

I see another issue in the June 2026 dispute over Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models: control over access.

I refer to [Anthropic's June 12 statement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access) for the company's account. It said the U.S. government issued an export-control directive to suspend access to both models for every foreign national, including employees. Anthropic disabled the models for all customers. The company understood that the government had learned of a method to bypass Fable 5's safeguards. Its statement did not provide the government's full evidence. I preserve that limit when I discuss the dispute.

I distinguish this episode from Anthropic's earlier conflict with the Pentagon over military uses and safeguards. The disputes concern different actions, legal questions, and immediate causes. Both raise questions about limits on powerful models.

I see access to advanced AI as a source of institutional power. Laboratories set conditions for use. Governments can invoke national-security authority. Employers decide which workers receive tools and who carries responsibility when systems fail. Workers and the public may have little influence over these choices.

I therefore examine AI governance through economic participation, professional autonomy, due process, national sovereignty, and the distribution of productive capacity, as well as model safety.

## What augmentation should mean in practice

I use augmentation to mean support that helps people do their work. I want institutions to attach measurable commitments to the phrase “Human–AI collaboration”.

In medicine, executives, insurers, clinical leaders, and regulators decide how to use AI. I want these systems to reduce clerical work and give doctors and nurses more time with patients. A useful system should preserve a named human decision-maker, record errors, and show improved outcomes across relevant populations. I would not judge success by processing speed alone.

In education, school boards, ministries, administrators, vendors, teachers, and parents shape how schools use AI. I would measure learning, teacher workload, student privacy, access, and opportunities for independent reasoning.

In software engineering, executives, product leaders, managers, customers, and security teams shape the effects on employment and quality. I would track defects, security vulnerabilities, incident recovery, review workload, junior hiring, mentorship, delivery time, and employee hours. The volume of generated code tells me little about whether an engineering organization is becoming stronger.

I also want workers to participate before institutions select a system. Consultation after that decision gives workers little power. Employees and professional bodies should help select tools, design work processes, assess risks, monitor performance, and decide how to share productivity gains.

## A future I can measure

I want institutions to treat productivity gains as a shared benefit. I see risks when executives receive rewards for lower labor costs and investors demand rapid profit growth. Governments may concentrate access, while organizations pass risks to workers and the public.

I want AI to make difficult work easier and valuable services more available. I also want responsible professionals to retain authority, dignity, bargaining power, and a fair share of the gains.

I would use these questions to judge progress:

1. Do productivity gains lead to higher wages, shorter hours, better services, or wider access?
2. Do employers still hire and train junior workers and give them a route to expertise?
3. Does a named person or institution remain accountable for decisions that affect people's lives?
4. Do institutions publicly measure errors, review costs, safety incidents, and unequal outcomes?
5. Do workers help decide how to use AI and receive credible retraining before their roles change?
6. Can customers, researchers, and governments examine access restrictions through transparent procedures?
7. Does economic power become more widely shared or more concentrated among model owners and institutions that control computing resources?

I use these questions to connect policy with decisions about work. I will judge the outcome by who keeps a job, gains a voice, carries the risk, and owns the productivity gains.
