# AI Utopia or Dystopia? The Answer Will Be Written in Employment Contracts

The future of work is often described as though artificial intelligence were a weather system moving toward us. Jobs will disappear. Productivity will rise. Entire professions will change. Workers are told to prepare for impact.

That language hides the people making the consequential decisions. Models do not lay off employees, redesign hospitals, reduce teaching staff, or remove junior engineering positions. Executives, boards, investors, public agencies, and managers decide how a new capability enters an institution. They choose whether productivity gains expand human capacity, shorten working hours, improve services, or flow mainly into margins and market power.

This is where my fear about AI begins. I worry that corporations will use automation to sever workers from the value they helped create. A company may discover that an AI-assisted team can produce more, then respond by reducing the team until the workload becomes intolerable. Another company may preserve headcount, invest in training, and let employees use the same tools to solve harder problems. Both organizations can claim to be adopting AI. Their social consequences will differ because their incentives and decisions differ.

The distinction matters now because the frontier AI industry is sending two messages at once. Leading laboratories compete aggressively for scarce human expertise. At the same time, their executives predict that AI systems will perform an expanding share of professional work.

## Follow the talent, carefully

The available evidence supports the narrower description **talent migration**. “Exodus” would imply a comprehensive, one-directional flight across the industry.

SignalFire’s [2025 State of Tech Talent report](https://www.signalfire.com/blog/signalfire-state-of-talent-report-2025) offers one window into this movement. In its dataset, engineers were eight times more likely to move from OpenAI to Anthropic than in the reverse direction; the DeepMind-to-Anthropic ratio was nearly eleven to one. Those figures describe SignalFire’s selected population and period, without mapping every hire, motive, or destination.

Retention is a separate measure. SignalFire reported that 80% of Anthropic employees who had been hired at least two years earlier remained through the end of their second year. The corresponding figures were 78% for DeepMind and 67% for OpenAI. This dataset-specific result suggests that Anthropic was retaining employees effectively under that definition. It cannot, by itself, establish why people stayed or whether the same pattern continued into 2026.

Frontier laboratories still place enormous value on experienced researchers and engineers who define problems, evaluate evidence, discover failure modes, and take responsibility for uncertain systems. Human judgment remains a strategic asset inside institutions building tools that may automate more intellectual labor.

That tension deserves more scrutiny than a slogan about machines taking jobs.

## What Amodei actually predicted

At the World Economic Forum in Davos on January 20, 2026, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said that some engineers at his company let the model produce code and then edit it or handle the surrounding work. He predicted that models might be six to twelve months away from doing “most, maybe all” of what software engineers do end to end. The [World Economic Forum’s account](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/young-people-ai-davos/) preserves both the prediction and his description of Anthropic’s internal practice.

That forecast concerns model capability and workflow. Measured displacement requires employment data. Code generation, task completion, occupational demand, and employment are connected, yet they remain different variables. A model can write a larger share of code while engineers shift toward specification, review, security, architecture, customer communication, and accountability. Employers may also use the same capability to shrink teams. Technology permits several organizational responses.

Anthropic’s [company-produced study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic) surveyed 132 engineers and researchers, conducted 53 interviews, and analyzed internal Claude Code usage. Employees self-reported using Claude in roughly 60% of their work and estimated an average productivity increase of about 50%. These observations from an unusually AI-intensive workplace should be compared with independent productivity, software-quality, working-hour, incident, and skill-development measures.

The early labor-market evidence is even more limited. An Anthropic analysis using U.S. Current Population Survey data found no systematic increase in unemployment among workers in highly AI-exposed occupations. For workers aged 22 to 25, it estimated a 14% decline in the rate of finding jobs in highly exposed occupations after ChatGPT’s release, compared with 2022. The authors called the estimate [“just barely statistically significant”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts), identified several alternative explanations, and described the overall hiring evidence as tentative. The analysis leaves causation unresolved.

Entry-level positions are how professions reproduce themselves. If firms automate junior tasks without creating new routes into expertise, short-term savings could weaken the future supply of senior professionals.

## Fable and the power to control access

The June 2026 dispute over Anthropic’s Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models introduces another part of the same political problem: concentrated control over access.

According to [Anthropic’s June 12 statement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access), the U.S. government issued an export-control directive requiring the company to suspend access to both models for every foreign national, including employees. Anthropic disabled the models for all customers. The company said its understanding was that the government had learned of a method for bypassing Fable 5’s safeguards; its statement did not provide the government’s full evidentiary case.

This episode should remain separate from Anthropic’s earlier conflict with the Pentagon over military uses and safeguards. The disputes involve different actions, legal questions, and immediate triggers, even though both concern the boundaries placed on powerful models.

Fable matters because access to advanced AI is becoming a lever of institutional power. Laboratories set deployment conditions; governments can invoke national-security authority; employers decide which workers receive powerful tools and who bears responsibility when systems fail. Workers and the public may have little influence over those choices.

AI governance therefore reaches beyond model safety. It includes economic participation, professional autonomy, due process, national sovereignty, and the distribution of productive capacity.

## What augmentation should mean in practice

“Human–AI collaboration” can become another empty corporate phrase unless institutions attach it to measurable commitments.

In medicine, hospital executives, insurers, clinical leaders, and regulators decide whether AI reduces clerical burden or becomes a justification for thinner staffing. A useful clinical system should give doctors and nurses more time with patients, preserve a named human decision-maker, document errors, and demonstrate improved outcomes across relevant populations. Faster processing alone is an inadequate success measure.

In education, school boards, ministries, administrators, vendors, teachers, and parents decide how AI enters classrooms. Success should be measured through learning, teacher workload, student privacy, access, and opportunities to develop independent reasoning.

In software engineering, executives, product leaders, engineering managers, customers, and security teams decide how coding agents affect employment and quality. A serious evaluation should track defect rates, security vulnerabilities, incident recovery, review burden, junior hiring, mentorship, delivery time, and employee hours. Lines of generated code reveal very little about whether an engineering organization is becoming stronger.

Workers also need standing in these decisions. Consultation after a deployment has already been chosen offers little power. Employees and professional bodies should participate in procurement, workflow design, risk assessment, performance monitoring, and decisions about how productivity gains are shared.

## A future we can measure

A humane AI economy requires institutions that treat productivity as a shared social gain. A darker outcome can emerge through ordinary incentives: executives rewarded for reducing labor costs, investors seeking rapid margin growth, governments concentrating access under broad authority, and organizations transferring risk downward to workers and the public.

I want AI to make difficult work easier and valuable services more available. I also want the people who carry professional responsibility to retain authority, dignity, bargaining power, and a fair share of the gains.

We can judge progress with concrete standards:

1. Are productivity gains accompanied by higher wages, shorter hours, better services, or broader access?
2. Are junior workers still being hired, trained, and given a path toward expertise?
3. Does a named person or institution remain accountable for consequential decisions?
4. Are error rates, review costs, safety incidents, and unequal outcomes measured publicly?
5. Do workers participate in deployment decisions and receive credible retraining before their roles change?
6. Can customers, researchers, and governments scrutinize restrictions on access through transparent procedures?
7. Is economic power becoming more widely distributed, or more concentrated among model owners and the institutions that control compute?

These measures turn a vague argument about destiny into a contest over policy and institutional design. Humanity still has choices to make. The outcome will be visible in who keeps a job, who gains a voice, who carries the risk, and who owns the productivity.
