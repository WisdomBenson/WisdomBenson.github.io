# What a model leaves out

Every successful calculation begins with an act of exclusion. We choose a boundary, freeze some degrees of freedom, approximate an interaction, and decide which observable will count as evidence that the model has done its job.

The tension is not that models are incomplete. A model without exclusions would be the world repeated, not the world understood. The tension is that predictive control can tempt us to forget which exclusions made that control possible.

## Abstraction is a scientific achievement

In computational materials physics, a finite cluster, a pseudopotential, or a convergence threshold is not an embarrassment to be hidden. Each is a disciplined decision about which structure matters for the question at hand.

For a simple confinement picture, one might write

$$E_g(r)=E_{g,\mathrm{bulk}}+\frac{A}{r^2}-\frac{B}{r}.$$

The equation is useful because it refuses detail. It isolates a competition between confinement and Coulombic effects. Its clarity comes from what it leaves out.

## Prediction is not yet explanation

A model may reproduce a band edge while misidentifying the mechanism. Surface states, passivation, finite-size strain, or numerical choices can produce similar outputs for different reasons. This is why a persuasive workflow needs more than a final number: it needs convergence tests, structural checks, and contact with experiment.

The philosophical point is modest. Agreement between model and measurement is evidence of success, but the meaning of that success depends on which alternatives were ruled out.

## The boundary is part of the result

Good scientific communication states the boundary of a claim with the same care used to state the claim itself. A conceptual widget is not a DFT calculation. A converged calculation is not automatically an experimental explanation. A useful approximation is not a literal picture of the system.

These distinctions do not weaken a result. They tell the reader exactly what the result has earned.
